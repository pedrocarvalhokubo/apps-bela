## Blends and digraphs

ID: e3-blends

Hear the sounds • Escute os sons

A blend keeps the sounds of its consonants. A digraph uses two letters for one sound. Say the word slowly and listen, instead of counting only letters.

### Blends:

fl in flag, gr in grape, st in star, cl in clap and tr in train. You can hear each consonant.

### Digraphs:

sh in ship, ch in cheese, th in thin and ph in phone make one sound.

### More blends:

crab, flame, brick, plant, glad, drift and straw.

### Watch wh:

what and whale start with /w/ in many accents; whole starts with /h/. Do not assume every wh word sounds the same.

Lembre: Blend: hear each consonant. Digraph: two letters work as one sound.

### Aprofundamento

Em flag, ouvimos /f/ e /l/. Em ship, sh representa um único som. Compare as palavras em voz alta: flag / ship; train / cheese.

Sort the words by the highlighted letter group, not by the whole word. A word can contain more than one interesting group. Practise with fl, gr, st, cl, tr, sh, ch, th and ph.

- blend: consonant sounds stay separate
- digraph: two letters represent one sound
- sound: what you hear

Exemplo: flag → fl → blend. ship → sh → digraph. photo → ph → digraph.

Desafio: Sort these words: crab, cheese, plant, shower, drift, photo. Then explain your choices aloud.

## Verbs

ID: e3-verbs

Actions and states • Ações e estados

A verb tells what someone or something does, or describes a state of being. Runs is an action; is can connect someone to a description.

### Action:

The girl runs. The dog eats. The children play.

### State of being:

He is happy. They are ready. I am calm.

### Find the verb:

Ask: what does the subject do? Or what word connects the subject to its state?

### Read in context:

In “She walks quietly”, walks is the verb and quietly tells how she walks.

Lembre: A verb can show an action OR a state of being.

### Aprofundamento

Não procure apenas movimentos. Am, is e are também são verbos. Em “He is happy”, happy descreve a pessoa; is é o verbo.

The word changes its job with the sentence. In “We play”, play is a verb. To identify a verb, read the whole sentence and find the action or state.

- run: an action
- is: a form of be
- subject: who or what the sentence is about

Exemplo: The children play happily. Who? The children. Do what? Play. How? Happily.

Desafio: Write one sentence with an action verb and one with am, is or are.

## Adverbs of manner

ID: e3-adverbs

How does it happen? • Como acontece?

An adverb can describe a verb, an adjective or another adverb. This review practises adverbs of manner: words that tell HOW an action happens.

### Ask how:

The turtle moves slowly. Slowly describes moves.

### Often add -ly:

slow → slowly; calm → calmly; beautiful → beautifully; loud → loudly.

### Change y to i:

easy → easily; happy → happily; lazy → lazily.

### Not always -ly:

good → well; hard can stay hard. In “She works hard”, hard is the adverb.

Lembre: Find the action, then ask HOW. Check the spelling!

### Aprofundamento

Compare: “a careful girl” describes a noun. “The girl walks carefully” describes the action. Choose an adverb to answer how she walks.

Context matters: a gymnast balances carefully, a sloth climbs slowly, a hungry dog eats hungrily and children wait patiently. Do not choose only by the -ly ending; the meaning must fit.

Quickly, quietly, gracefully, bravely, gently, calmly, honestly and patiently describe different ways of acting. Practise turning an adjective into an adverb, then using it in a sentence.

- slowly: devagar
- quietly: sem fazer barulho
- patiently: com paciência

Exemplo: Happy describes a person: a happy boy. Happily describes an action: the boy played happily.

Desafio: Complete: easy → ___; lazy → ___; beautiful → ___. Then write a sentence with carefully.

## Homophones

ID: e3-homophones

Same sound, different words • Mesmo som

Homophones sound the same but have different meanings and, in the pairs studied here, different spellings. Read the sentence to choose the meaning you need.

### People and things:

son / sun; pair / pear; flower / flour.

### Actions and places:

see / sea; meet / meat; knew / new; wear / where.

### Useful contrasts:

right / write; road / rode; paws / pause; weak / week; steel / steal.

### Three-way clues:

two = 2; too = also or more than needed; to = direction or part of an infinitive. Their shows possession; they’re means they are; there indicates a place.

Lembre: The sound is a clue. The sentence decides the meaning.

### Aprofundamento

Use meaning first. “The lion has big paws” needs the name of its feet. “Take a pause” needs a word for a short stop. They sound alike but cannot be swapped.

Check their / they’re / there by replacing they’re with they are. “Their hats” means the hats belong to them. “Over there” points to a place.

Read this practice passage: “I knew it would be a good day. The sky was blue. I went to meet my friend by the sea. We saw two ducks. At home, we used flour to bake a cake. I write in my journal.” Each choice depends on meaning.

- sea / see: ocean / look with your eyes
- pear / pair: fruit / two matching things
- write / right: put words on paper / correct or a direction

Exemplo: Their kite is over there. They’re happy. Replace they’re with they are to check.

Desafio: Write eight homophone pairs. Use two pairs in sentences that make their meanings clear.

## Homographs and homonyms

ID: e3-homographs

One spelling, different meanings • O contexto decide

Homographs have the same spelling but different meanings; pronunciation can be the same or different. The examples in your review also share the same pronunciation and are called homonyms in the worksheet.

### bat:

a flying mammal OR an object used to hit a ball.

### palm and wave:

palm: part of a hand or a tree. wave: moving water or a greeting gesture.

### bark, nail, ring:

bark: dog sound / tree covering; nail: fingernail / metal fastener; ring: jewellery / a bell’s sound or the action.

### More examples:

hand: body part / clock pointer; ball: toy / formal dance; seal: animal / close securely; light: brightness / not heavy; fair: event / just.

Lembre: Same spelling does not guarantee the same meaning. Read the context.

### Aprofundamento

A sentence tells which meaning fits. “The bat flew at night” suggests the animal. “She hit the ball with a bat” suggests sports equipment.

The worksheet uses the heading homograph and the word homonym. Keep the distinction clear: same spelling defines a homograph. The examples palm, bat and wave also have the same pronunciation.

For a paired-sentence exercise, the SAME word must fit both gaps. Check both sentences before choosing. “Put your hand up” and “The clock hand points to twelve” use one spelling with different meanings.

- bat: animal / sports equipment
- bark: tree covering / dog sound
- light: brightness / not heavy

Exemplo: The dog can bark. The tree has rough bark. Same spelling and sound; different meanings.

Desafio: Write two sentences for each word: nail, ring, light and fair. Show a different meaning in each sentence.

## Fiction and non-fiction

ID: e3-genres

Imagine or inform? • A intenção do texto

Fiction presents imagined stories. Non-fiction gives information about real people, places, events or ideas. Look at the purpose and evidence, not just whether the book has pictures.

### Fiction:

Invented characters and events; often a setting, plot, problem and solution. It may entertain or communicate a message.

### Not always magic:

A realistic made-up story is still fiction. Fiction may use real places.

### Non-fiction:

Uses facts and real information to teach or explain. Examples include biographies, textbooks and informational articles.

### Ask why:

Is the author mainly telling an imagined story or explaining real information? Both types can have illustrations.

Lembre: Fiction: imagined story. Non-fiction: information grounded in reality.

### Aprofundamento

A story about a dragon who borrows a library book is fiction. A factual article explaining how a library works is non-fiction. The subject alone does not decide the genre.

Novels, folktales, pourquoi stories and short stories can be fiction. Biographies, news reports and factual textbooks are non-fiction. A pourquoi story gives an imaginative explanation for why something is the way it is.

Some non-fiction texts tell real events as a story. So do not decide only from “beginning, middle and end”. Check whether the events are presented as factual or invented.

- fiction: imagined story
- non-fiction: real information
- purpose: why the author writes

Exemplo: A talking spider opening a café → fiction. An article about spider habitats → non-fiction.

Desafio: Choose a book or a paragraph. Name its genre and point to two clues that support your choice.

## Informational text features

ID: e3-informational

Find your way through facts • Recursos do texto

Informational texts teach readers about a topic. Features such as titles, subheadings, pictures, captions and glossaries make facts easier to find and understand.

### Title:

Names or sums up the whole topic. A subheading introduces a smaller section.

### Paragraphs and lists:

Paragraphs group related information. Bullet points help list important details.

### Images and captions:

Photographs, illustrations and diagrams support information. A caption explains an image; a label names a part.

### Glossary:

Defines special words used in the text. Look there when a term is unfamiliar.

Lembre: Title = whole topic. Subheading = section. Caption = image. Glossary = word meaning.

### Aprofundamento

Read the title before the paragraph. Then use the subheading to predict what that section will explain. A paragraph about where spiders live needs a habitat subheading, not a food subheading.

Photographs show appearance. Diagrams can label body parts. Captions tell us what the image shows. None of these replaces reading the text carefully.

The worksheet practises spotting four features in “Spiders”: facts, photographs, subheadings and a glossary. An informational text need not contain every possible feature.

- subheading: section heading
- caption: text explaining a picture
- glossary: definitions of special words

Exemplo: Title: Spiders. Subheading: Food. Caption: A spider on its web. Glossary: prey = an animal hunted for food.

Desafio: Create a mini information page: title, two subheadings, three facts, a drawing with a caption, and one glossary entry.

## Biography and autobiography

ID: e3-biography

Who tells the life story? • Quem escreve?

A biography is a factual account of someone’s life written by another person. An autobiography is written by the person whose life it describes.

### Biography:

Usually uses third person: he, she or they. The author researches another person’s life.

### Autobiography:

Usually uses first person: I, me, my, we. Auto means self.

### Chronological order:

Important events are commonly organised from earliest to latest, using dates and sequence words.

### What to include:

Birth and childhood, influences, important events, achievements and relevant feelings or quotations. Explain why the person is remembered.

Lembre: Biography: another author. Autobiography: the person writes their own life.

### Aprofundamento

Compare these made-up practice sentences. Biography: “She learned to draw when she was six.” Autobiography: “I learned to draw when I was six.” The information can be similar, but the writer’s position changes.

A biography can be about a living person. It does not need to end with a death. An autobiography may describe a particular period rather than every moment of someone’s life.

Use dates as evidence of sequence. A quotation in a biography may contain I because the author quotes the subject; one pronoun alone is not enough to classify the whole text.

Uma autobiografia é um relato da própria pessoa; uma biografia depende da pesquisa de outro autor. Ambas devem ser lidas com atenção às fontes e ao ponto de vista.

- autobiography: self-written life story
- biography: life written by another person
- chronological: earliest to latest

Exemplo: First: born. Next: childhood. Later: work and achievements. Finally: later life or what the person does now.

Desafio: Write a short fictional practice autobiography using I. Rewrite two sentences as a biography using he, she or they.

## Reading: Roald Dahl

ID: e3-dahl

Read a life story • Encontre as evidências

Read the short biography below. Use dates, names and subheadings to locate answers. Explain each answer with information from the text.

### Early life:

Roald Dahl was born on 13 September 1916 in Llandaff, Wales. His parents were Norwegian. His mother’s stories helped shape his imagination.

### School:

He attended St Peter’s and later Repton. The study passage describes his skill in sports, including boxing, and his experience tasting chocolate.

### Work and writing:

He worked for an oil company, joined the RAF, and was injured in a plane crash in Libya in 1940. In 1942, C. S. Forester encouraged him to write about flying.

### Stories and legacy:

His children’s books include James and the Giant Peach, Charlie and the Chocolate Factory, The BFG, The Twits and Matilda. He died on 23 November 1990.

Lembre: For a reading question, find the sentence that supports your answer.

### Aprofundamento

EARLY LIFE — Roald Dahl was born in Wales in 1916. His parents came from Norway. His sister Astri died in 1920, and his father died soon afterwards. His mother told stories about trolls and magical creatures. These stories influenced his imagination.

SCHOOL AND WORK — He went to boarding school at nine and later studied at Repton. The reading passage describes boxing as one of his strengths. Tasting chocolates at school later helped inspire Charlie and the Chocolate Factory. After working for an oil company in Africa, he became an RAF pilot. He was injured in a crash in Libya in 1940.

BECOMING A WRITER — In Washington, D.C., in 1942, author C. S. Forester encouraged Dahl to describe his flying experiences. Dahl also told stories to his children. His family life and imagination contributed to his writing. Among his well-known books are James and the Giant Peach, The BFG and Matilda.

READING STRATEGY — Match 1916 to his birth, 1920 to Astri’s death and 1940 to the crash in Libya. For a personal achievement and a professional achievement, use examples such as raising a family and writing successful books. This review is an adaptation for comprehension, not a quotation of Dahl’s own autobiography.

- Wales: country of birth
- RAF: Royal Air Force
- achievement: something a person accomplishes

Exemplo: Question: Who encouraged him to write about flying? Evidence: “author C. S. Forester encouraged Dahl”. Answer with the name and the relevant detail.

Desafio: Write a timeline with three dates. Then explain how childhood chocolate tasting influenced one of his books.

## Reading: Spiders

ID: e3-spiders

Facts, headings and glossary • Leia para aprender

Read this informational text about spiders. Notice how each section groups related facts. Practise choosing subheadings and finding evidence, rather than memorising every number.

### Family and body:

Spiders are arachnids and invertebrates. They have eight legs, an exoskeleton and two main body regions: cephalothorax and abdomen.

### Habitats:

Some spiders live in burrows. Others shelter in gaps between bricks, among plants or in wood piles.

### Size:

Spiders come in many sizes. The Goliath birdeater is a large tarantula from northern South America.

### Food:

Many spiders eat insects. Some catch prey in webs; others hunt without a web. Many use venom to subdue prey.

Lembre: Find the main idea of each paragraph, then choose its subheading.

### Aprofundamento

FAMILY AND BODY — Spiders are arachnids. Unlike insects, which have six legs, spiders have eight. Their bodies have an outer skeleton called an exoskeleton. Their two main regions are the cephalothorax and abdomen.

HOMES — Trapdoor spiders live in burrows. Other spiders find shelter among vegetation, between bricks or in wood piles. Different species live in different habitats.

SIZE AND FOOD — Spiders vary greatly in size. The Goliath birdeater is a large tarantula. Many spiders catch insects for food. Web-building spiders can trap prey in silk; other spiders hunt. Many inject venom into prey.

GLOSSARY — Exoskeleton: a hard outer support and covering. Hairy: covered with hairs. Scuttle: move quickly with short steps. Prey: an animal hunted for food. Caption: words that explain a picture.

TEXT DETECTIVE — This is non-fiction because it presents real information about spiders. It has facts, subheadings and a glossary. A photograph with an explanatory caption would add visual information. A talking spider with an invented adventure would be fiction.

- arachnid: group that includes spiders
- exoskeleton: hard outer support and covering
- prey: animal hunted for food

Exemplo: “Some spiders live in burrows; others shelter between bricks.” Best subheading: Homes or Habitats, because both sentences explain where spiders live.

Desafio: Write five facts from the text. Then propose subheadings for homes, size, body and food, and define exoskeleton in your own words.

## Ampliação curada

Exemplos autorais: compare The turtle moved slowly e The turtle moved carefully. O verbo descreve a ação; o advérbio muda como a imaginamos. Nem todo advérbio termina em -ly: fast pode descrever o modo. Evite a regra incorreta de que toda palavra em -ly é advérbio.

Em homophones, use o contexto de uma frase para decidir o sentido, não apenas o som. Em homographs, a mesma escrita pode ter sentidos diferentes; verifique a pronúncia no caso concreto. Não exija uma definição mais técnica do que a trabalhada na aula.

Para comparar gêneros, imagine uma página com instruções de cuidado de uma planta e uma narrativa fictícia em que a planta conversa. O tema pode ser igual; a finalidade e as evidências mudam.

Ao comentar Roald Dahl ou aranhas, cite apenas fatos presentes na aula. Distinguir opinião, previsão e informação explícita é mais útil do que decorar frases.
